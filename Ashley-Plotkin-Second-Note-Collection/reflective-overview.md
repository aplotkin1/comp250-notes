# Reflective Overview — Second Note Collection

**Research Question:** How should generative AI use be regulated differently in K–8, high school, and higher education to protect student learning and development?

For my second note collection, I focused on understanding how generative AI affects students at different educational levels and what kinds of rules might help protect their learning. I selected four secondary sources that examine AI in K–12 education, risks for younger students, learning outcomes in higher education, and the responsibilities of colleges when introducing AI into academic work.

I began by researching younger students because I believe they are the most vulnerable to becoming dependent on AI. Alfarwan (2025) helped me understand that there is still limited research involving elementary school students. This made me question whether schools should introduce AI to younger children before researchers fully understand its effects. Chang and Razi (2026) also discussed concerns about students trusting AI-generated information and potentially losing opportunities to develop independent thinking. These findings supported my original belief that younger students need stronger restrictions while they develop foundational skills.

My research then shifted toward higher education. Chen and Cheung (2025) found that generative AI can have positive effects on university students' learning outcomes. This was probably the most surprising finding for me because I often think negatively about AI and worry that students rely on it too much. However, I also use AI myself, and it has helped me study, understand difficult material, and perform well on exams. Thinking about my own experiences made me realize that AI is not necessarily harmful to learning. Its impact depends on how students use it. There is a difference between asking AI to explain a concept and asking it to complete an assignment.

My final notes focused on responsible AI use in college. McGrath, Farazouli, and Cerratto-Pargman (2025) helped me consider academic integrity, verifying AI-generated information, and fairness. I believe professors should clearly explain what students are allowed to use AI for. I also think students need to double-check AI responses, especially when researching information or writing code. Another issue I considered was unequal access to paid AI tools. Although I do not believe colleges need to provide every student with paid subscriptions, they should provide access when a specific tool is required for a class.

The subject terms I used throughout my notes, including *critical thinking*, *AI dependence*, *independent learning*, and *responsible AI use*, helped me identify connections between the sources. My opinion has not changed, but the research has helped me explain it more clearly. I still believe K–8 students need stronger restrictions, high school students should approach AI cautiously, and college students should be allowed to use it responsibly. Moving forward, I want to investigate more specific examples of age-appropriate AI policies, especially for high school students, and examine how schools can encourage learning without making students dependent on technology.
