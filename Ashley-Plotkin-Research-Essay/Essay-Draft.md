# To the Reader

# Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly common in education, changing how students study, complete assignments, and understand difficult concepts. Tools such as ChatGPT can generate ideas, explanations, and offer academic assistance within seconds. Although these tools can be useful, they raise concerns about whether students are developing their own academic skills or becoming too dependent on technology. This issue is especially important because students at different educational levels have different learning needs and levels of independence. A tool that helps a college student understand a difficult concept might prevent a younger student from practicing the skills needed to solve problems independently. 

Research on generative AI in education identifies both potential benefits and risks. @alfarwan2025 looks at the use of generative AI in K-12 education, while @changrazi2026 identify concerns involving independent thinking and the reliability of AI-generated information among younger users. Meanwhile, @chencheung2025 report positive effects on university students' learning outcomes. These findings raise an important question about whether schools should apply the same AI policies to students of all ages or establish different rules based on students' educational levels.

Using the same AI policies across all educational levels could create problems. Allowing unrestricted AI use might encourage younger students to rely on generated answers before developing essential academic skills. However, banning AI entirely could prevent older students from benefiting from technology that is becoming increasingly relevant to education and future careers. Generative AI therefore requires different levels of regulation across education: stronger restrictions in K-8 to protect foundational learning, supervised and clearly defined use in high school, and responsible-use guidelines in higher education to balance academic integrity with the benefits of emerging technology. 

# Benefits of Generative AI in Education

Generative AI offers several benefits in education, particularly through personalized learning and additional academic support. Students learn at different speeds, and some may need more time or different explanations to understand certain concepts. Generative AI allows students to ask questions, receive immediate feedback, and explore difficult material at their own pace. For example, a student struggling with a mathematical concept can ask AI to provide a simpler explanation or generate similar practice problems. This can be especially helpful when a teacher or professor is not immediately available. Rather than replacing traditional classroom instruction, AI can provide an additional resource that supports students outside the classroom.

Research also suggests that generative AI can positively influence students' academic performance. @chencheung2025 [10] found positive effects on university students' learning outcomes, including academic achievement and higher-order thinking skills. These findings suggest that generative AI can support more than simply completing assignments faster. When students actively engage with the material, AI can help them explore different approaches to problems and strengthen their understanding. However, these benefits depend on how students interact with the technology. Asking AI to explain a difficult concept is different from asking it to complete an entire assignment.

Generative AI can also benefit teachers by helping them prepare educational materials and develop different ways to explain challenging topics. @alfarwan2025 [sec. 4.3] discusses the potential of generative AI to support teaching and learning while emphasizing the importance of teacher decision-making. Teachers can use AI to create practice questions, develop lesson plans, and provide additional learning resources. This allows younger students to benefit indirectly from AI without necessarily using the technology themselves.

Despite these advantages, students' perceptions of learning do not always reflect their actual understanding. @alfarwan2025 [sec. 4.5] raises concerns about studies that rely on students' self-reported learning experiences. A student might understand an AI-generated explanation but still struggle to solve a similar problem independently. Therefore, the effectiveness of generative AI should be evaluated not only by how helpful students find it, but also by what they can accomplish without it. Recognizing this distinction is particularly important when considering whether younger students are ready to use generative AI independently.

# Risks to K–8 Student Development

Elementary and middle school are important stages in students' academic development. During these years, students are learning how to read, write, communicate, think critically, and solve problems. These skills provide the foundation for more advanced learning in high school and college. Introducing generative AI too early could interfere with this development by reducing opportunities for students to practice these skills independently. For example, if an elementary school student regularly uses AI to generate written responses, the student may miss opportunities to develop vocabulary, organize ideas, and express thoughts in their own words. Although the completed assignment might meet the teacher's expectations, it may not accurately represent the student's abilities.

In their systematic literature review of generative AI risks for youth, @changrazi2026 [3] explain that while generative AI could improve short-term performance, it could also "erode independent thinking," "fragment ideation," and "reduce retention." This raises concerns about the long-term effects of introducing AI during the early stages of education. Struggling with a difficult assignment is not necessarily a negative experience because it allows students to develop patience, confidence, and problem-solving skills. If students become accustomed to receiving immediate answers, they may become less willing to work through challenges on their own.

Another concern is the reliability of AI-generated information. @changrazi2026 [3] found that young users often accepted AI-generated responses without verifying their accuracy and experienced difficulties identifying misinformation. Younger students may assume that an answer is correct simply because it sounds confident or is presented clearly. Before relying on AI-generated information, students need opportunities to learn how to evaluate sources, recognize inaccurate information, and question what they read.

There is also limited research specifically examining generative AI use among elementary school students. @alfarwan2025 [sec. 3.1.2] identifies greater representation of middle school students than elementary school students in the existing literature. This research gap makes it difficult to determine the long-term consequences of introducing generative AI at an early age. Schools should not assume that benefits observed among older students will automatically apply to younger children.

For these reasons, independent generative AI use should be strongly restricted in K–8 education, particularly at the elementary level. Younger students need sufficient opportunities to develop foundational academic skills before incorporating tools that can perform many of these tasks for them. Establishing these skills early can better prepare students to use AI responsibly as they progress into high school and higher education.

# Responsible AI Use in High School

High school represents a transition between foundational education and the greater academic independence expected in higher education. Unlike elementary school students, high school students have had more opportunities to develop their reading, writing, and problem-solving skills. However, they are still developing these abilities and preparing for the academic responsibilities of college. Introducing generative AI during high school can help students become familiar with emerging technology, but its use requires clear boundaries to prevent academic dependence.

In a study involving interviews with 18 high school students, @chanenson2026 examined students' experiences with ChatGPT and school AI policies. Twelve participants were unaware of specific school rules regarding ChatGPT. Students also experienced inconsistencies in how teachers permitted and enforced AI use. An activity considered acceptable in one classroom could be considered academic dishonesty in another. Although the study involved a relatively small group of students during the early adoption of ChatGPT, its findings highlight the difficulties students face when schools fail to communicate clear expectations.

The researchers also identified problems with AI detection and monitoring. Students expressed concerns about being falsely accused of using AI, while others found ways to avoid detection. Relying heavily on detection tools can shift students' attention toward avoiding punishment rather than understanding why certain uses of AI are academically inappropriate. Schools need to communicate the educational purpose behind their restrictions instead of focusing exclusively on catching students who violate them. Involving students in discussions about AI policies could also help schools develop more practical and understandable expectations.

High school students should gradually develop AI literacy, including an understanding of how generative AI works, its limitations, and the importance of verifying its responses. Teachers could introduce supervised activities in which students evaluate AI-generated information, identify errors, or compare different approaches to solving a problem. However, assignments designed to assess independent writing, reasoning, and problem-solving should remain restricted from unauthorized AI assistance.

Preparing students for college involves more than teaching them how to use emerging technologies. Students must also develop the ability to complete challenging academic tasks without depending on those technologies. Allowing limited and supervised AI use in high school provides an opportunity to develop responsible technology habits while protecting the academic skills students will need in higher education.

# Generative AI in Higher Education

Generative AI has a different role in higher education because college students are expected to take greater responsibility for their own learning. Unlike younger students, college students have generally developed foundational academic skills and are applying them to more advanced subjects. Generative AI can support this process by helping students understand complex material, explore unfamiliar topics, and prepare for future careers. However, greater academic independence also means that students must take responsibility for how they use the technology.

A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by @chencheung2025 examined 57 studies and 97 effect size estimates involving generative AI in higher education. The researchers reported a large overall effect size of 0.804 on university students' learning outcomes. This provides evidence that generative AI can contribute positively to college education. Rather than banning the technology entirely, universities can recognize its educational potential while establishing expectations that protect academic integrity.

As a college student studying computer science, I have experienced some of these benefits myself. Generative AI has helped me understand difficult programming concepts, prepare for exams, and identify errors in my code. However, receiving a working solution does not necessarily mean that I understand how the solution works. Testing the code, reviewing its logic, and explaining the reasoning behind it are still important parts of the learning process. This experience illustrates how AI can support academic development without replacing the student's responsibilities.

Accuracy is another important consideration in higher education. @mcgrath2025 [1542] discuss the importance of validating chatbot-generated material. College students who use AI for research must verify information and confirm that academic sources actually exist. Similarly, students using AI for programming must test generated code rather than assuming that it is correct. Regardless of whether AI contributes to an assignment, students remain responsible for the accuracy and quality of their submitted work.

Universities also need to establish clear expectations regarding acceptable AI use. Different courses have different learning objectives, so professors should have the flexibility to determine which forms of AI assistance are appropriate for their assignments. For example, AI-assisted debugging might be permitted in a programming course, while generating an entire essay might violate the requirements of a writing assignment. Students should also disclose their AI use when required. These guidelines allow universities to benefit from emerging technology while maintaining academic integrity and encouraging independent learning.

# Critical Thinking and Learning Outcomes

When evaluating the effectiveness of generative AI in education, it is important to consider what students actually learn rather than focusing only on their academic performance. Receiving a higher grade does not necessarily mean that a student has developed stronger critical-thinking or problem-solving skills. Education involves learning how to analyze information, develop arguments, recognize mistakes, and apply knowledge to unfamiliar situations. These abilities are important regardless of a student's educational level.

@chencheung2025 found that generative AI had a medium effect size of 0.580 on higher-order thinking among university students. However, the researchers found no statistically significant effect on metacognition, which refers to students' ability to understand and manage their own learning. This distinction is important because students need to recognize when they understand a concept and when they require additional practice. Although AI can provide explanations and feedback, students must still evaluate their own understanding and identify areas where they need improvement.

The researchers also acknowledge that the benefits of generative AI for higher-order thinking may have been overestimated because many interventions were short-term and relied on self-reported data [@chencheung2025, 15]. These limitations raise questions about whether improvements observed during AI-assisted activities will continue when students no longer have access to the technology. Long-term learning requires students to retain knowledge and apply it independently, rather than relying on assistance every time they encounter a difficult problem.

One way educators could address this concern is by assessing students through both AI-assisted and independent activities. For example, students could use AI to explore a topic and then complete an assessment without technological assistance. Teachers could also ask students to explain their reasoning, identify mistakes in AI-generated responses, or demonstrate how they reached a conclusion. These activities would help educators determine whether AI is contributing to students' understanding or simply improving the quality of their submitted work.

Ultimately, the educational value of generative AI depends on whether it strengthens students' abilities rather than replaces them. Evaluating independent learning outcomes can help schools and universities determine which uses of AI are appropriate for students at different stages of their education.

# Student Privacy and Educational Guidelines

In addition to protecting students' academic development, educational institutions must consider the privacy and security risks associated with generative AI. Students may not fully understand what happens to the information they enter into AI tools or how that information could be collected and used. These concerns are particularly important for younger students, who may be less aware of the consequences of sharing personal information online. Schools have a responsibility to evaluate AI tools before introducing them into educational environments.

Chicago Public Schools provides an example of how educational institutions can establish guidelines for responsible AI use. Its student AI guidance permits only district-approved, age-appropriate generative AI tools that satisfy privacy and security requirements. Students must also receive permission from their teachers before using these tools for academic work [@cpsai]. This approach places responsibility on both educators and students rather than allowing students to independently determine which tools are appropriate.

An important distinction in the CPS guidance is that approval based on privacy and security requirements does not guarantee that an AI tool satisfies academic expectations concerning accuracy, bias, or fairness [@cpsai]. A tool may protect students' personal information while still generating inaccurate or misleading responses. Therefore, schools must consider both the technical safety of AI tools and their educational effectiveness when deciding whether students should use them.

The district also emphasizes academic integrity by requiring students to identify and disclose their use of AI-generated material. Its guidance recognizes potential educational uses, such as brainstorming, generating practice questions, and receiving feedback, while maintaining expectations for students' original work [@cpsai]. These guidelines demonstrate that schools can acknowledge the benefits of generative AI without permitting unrestricted use.

Accessibility is another consideration, particularly in higher education. @mcgrath2025 [1546] raise concerns about potential inequalities among students using generative AI. Students who can afford paid subscriptions may have access to features unavailable to other students. However, educational institutions do not necessarily need to provide every student with a paid AI subscription. If a professor requires a particular AI tool for an assignment, the institution should ensure that all students have access to the necessary resources.

Although the CPS guidelines establish a useful framework for responsible AI use, educational institutions should further consider how these expectations differ according to students' ages and academic development. Protecting privacy, maintaining academic integrity, and ensuring fair access are important across all educational levels, but the degree of independence students receive when using AI should reflect their educational needs.

# Counterargument and Age-Appropriate AI Guidelines

One argument against restricting generative AI in K–12 education is that students need early exposure to emerging technologies to prepare for their future academic and professional responsibilities. As AI becomes increasingly common in education and the workplace, limiting access could prevent students from developing important technological skills. Supporters of early AI integration might also argue that generative AI can provide personalized academic assistance to students who need additional support outside the classroom. These concerns are reasonable because schools have a responsibility to prepare students for a changing technological environment.

However, learning about artificial intelligence does not necessarily require students to use generative AI independently. Teachers can introduce younger students to basic AI concepts, explain how the technology works, and demonstrate its limitations without allowing it to complete academic tasks for them. This approach would help students become familiar with emerging technology while continuing to develop their foundational skills. The objective of restricting AI in younger grades is not to prevent technological advancement but to ensure that students are academically prepared to use these tools responsibly.

In K–8 education, independent generative AI use should be strongly restricted, particularly in elementary school. Teachers can use approved AI tools to develop educational materials and provide additional learning resources, but students should primarily complete reading, writing, and problem-solving activities independently. Middle school students could gradually participate in teacher-led demonstrations that introduce AI literacy and online safety without encouraging dependence on generated answers.

In high school, students should be permitted to use generative AI for specific educational purposes under clearly communicated guidelines. Acceptable activities could include generating practice questions, receiving feedback, and exploring different explanations of difficult concepts. However, students should not use AI to complete assignments intended to assess their independent abilities unless the teacher specifically authorizes such assistance. Schools should also teach students how to verify AI-generated information and disclose their use of the technology.

In higher education, universities should allow greater flexibility while maintaining academic integrity requirements. Professors should communicate acceptable AI practices according to the learning objectives of each course. Students should remain responsible for verifying information, understanding their submitted work, and acknowledging AI assistance when required. Independent assessments should also remain part of higher education to ensure that students are developing the knowledge and abilities necessary for their future careers.

These recommendations provide a gradual transition from foundational learning to responsible technological independence. Instead of applying identical restrictions to every student, educational institutions can adjust their expectations as students develop greater academic maturity. Such an approach recognizes the potential benefits of generative AI without overlooking the importance of independent learning at every educational level.

# Conclusion

Generative AI presents both opportunities and challenges for education, making it difficult to establish a single policy that meets the needs of students at every educational level. Although research demonstrates that AI can improve certain learning outcomes, concerns about academic dependence, independent thinking, privacy, and academic integrity cannot be ignored. These concerns are particularly important when considering the differences in academic development between younger students and college students.

An age-appropriate approach to AI regulation provides a balance between protecting student learning and preparing students for a future involving artificial intelligence. Stronger restrictions in K–8 education can protect the development of foundational academic skills, while supervised use in high school can help students develop responsible technology habits. In higher education, greater flexibility can allow students to benefit from AI while remaining accountable for their academic work. Across all educational levels, the primary purpose of using AI should be to support learning rather than replace it.

Future research should examine the long-term effects of generative AI on students' academic development, particularly among elementary and middle school students. Additional research could also help educators determine which AI-assisted activities contribute to independent learning and which encourage dependence. As generative AI continues to develop, educational institutions will need to regularly evaluate and update their policies based on new evidence.

Ultimately, the goal of education is not simply to help students complete assignments or achieve higher grades. It is to prepare them to think independently, solve problems, and apply their knowledge beyond the classroom. Generative AI should contribute to that goal rather than interfere with it.
